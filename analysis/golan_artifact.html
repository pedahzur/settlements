Consensus Without Settlers

The one arena Israel formally annexed is the slow lane of its settlement enterprise. The reason is not law or ideology but a map — the settlements that compound sit within a short commute of Tel Aviv and Jerusalem, and the Golan is two hours away.

In 1981 Israel extended its law to the Golan Heights; in 2019 the United States recognized the annexation. No other arena of the settlement project carries that political standing. Yet by the measure the project sets for itself — bodies on the ground — the Golan is where it has performed the worst. This piece asks why, and finds the answer on a road map.

Part I The slow lane

The Central Bureau of Statistics has published population for every Golan locality each year since 2003. Read across two decades, the file tells a story that legal status and ideology do not predict: the annexed highland grew slowly, its one town slowest of all, while the five Druze and Alawite towns it was meant to demographically overtake held their ground.

Figure 1 — The crossover

The gap that opened is thin. After annexation, American recognition, and half a century of settlement, the Jewish population passed the pre-1967 Druze and Alawite population only recently, and by a margin — about 2,300 people — smaller than the town of Katzrin. Why so slow? Because what drives the West Bank is absent here. The settlements that compound — the ultra-Orthodox cities, the commuter communities — have no equivalent on the Golan, whose form profile mirrors the Jordan Valley: moshavim and kibbutzim, one town, a handful of community settlements.

Part II The map decides

Israel has two economic gravity wells: Gush Dan, the Tel Aviv metropolis, and Greater Jerusalem. A settlement's demography turns less on its politics than on its distance — in driving time — from one of them. Where a settler can live in the hills and still reach an office in under 45 minutes, settlements fill and compound. Where the commute runs past an hour, they thin into villages.

The West Bank blocs sit inside that radius. The ultra-Orthodox cities of Modi'in Illit and Beitar Illit, the commuter suburb of Ma'ale Adumim — the enterprise's demographic engines — are 25 to 40 minutes from a major core. The Jordan Valley, though inside the West Bank, lies beyond it. The Golan is not in the conversation.

Figure 3 — Driving time to the economy

Driving time explains where large settlements can exist; it does not, by itself, set the growth rate of any single village. That distinction is the honest core of the argument. Plot every settlement in all three arenas by its distance to the nearest core against its size, and the pattern is not a slope but a ceiling: proximity does not make a village grow faster — it decides whether a village can ever become a city.

Figure 4 — Distance sets the ceiling

Proximity doesn't make a village grow faster. It decides whether a village can ever become a city — and only the West Bank has the geography for it.

The mechanism behind the map is work. The 2008 Census — the last to record place of work and industry at the regional-council level — profiles each council, and on those numbers the three arenas could not look more different.

Figure 5 — Place of work and economy

On the numbers. These are the actual 2008-census figures for each council, not estimates — the contrast is the argument. The two West Bank councils, north and south of Jerusalem, tell one story twice: almost no farmers (0.9% and 2.0%) and roughly three-quarters of the workforce commuting out to salaried jobs. The Golan and the Jordan Valley remain roughly one-fifth agricultural, older, and more self-employed. CBS has published no locality-level place-of-work product since 2008, so this is the most recent comparable reading. A useful corroborant from 2017 (CBS Local Authorities File, via the Jerusalem Institute): about 36,300 people commute from the West Bank into Jerusalem for work.

Part III Inside the Golan

Return to the annexed highland with the mechanism in hand. The growth that exists in the Jewish sector is real but small, and spread across many tiny bases rather than concentrated in a compounding core. A moshav that went from 47 residents to 770 posts a spectacular rate; it is still a village. Filter the 33 Jewish localities by settlement form, or sort by size against growth, and the pattern reads directly: the largest places grew slowest, and the fastest rates belong to the smallest.

Consensus, in Israeli politics, is what the Golan has: no serious party proposes returning it. Settlers are what it lacks. Geography and economics — not the annexation vote, not the recognition — decided the demography. Measured in driving time, the Golan was never within range of the machine that built the West Bank. The arena with the strongest political standing shows the weakest performance, and that inversion is the finding the comparative article is built to explain.
